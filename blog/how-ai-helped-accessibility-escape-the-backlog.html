I planned to write a different article than this one.

The original idea was straightforward. Talk about accessibility, cite the numbers, make the case that AI gives us time to finally address it, and move on. Clean thesis. Easy to write. Probably fine.

Then I spent the last few weeks doing more accessibility work than I have done in my entire career on a single product. And it changed what I wanted to say.

While Everyone Is Arguing About Jobs

The loudest conversation in AI right now is about what it is taking. Jobs. Careers. Industries. The concern is real and I am not here to dismiss it. I am as concerned as anyone about the environmental cost too. The energy consumption, the water draw, the questions we have not fully answered yet about what it costs the planet to run these systems at scale. These are not small things and they deserve serious attention.

But while we are working through those conundrums something else is happening that is not getting nearly enough attention. AI is giving things back. Specifically it is giving back time. And that recovered time gets to go towards all the things you always wanted to do but could never justify in a sprint.

Accessibility has always been at the top of that list.

The Thing That Always Got Pushed

If you have worked in product design for any length of time you know exactly how this goes. Accessibility work gets scoped. It gets estimated. Everyone agrees it matters. And then the sprint fills up with features and edge cases and the accessibility ticket moves to the next cycle. And the next one. And the one after that.

It is not that teams do not care. Most of the people I have worked with over thirty years of this career genuinely do. It is that time and budget have always been the constraint that wins. When you are choosing between shipping a feature a hundred thousand users will see immediately and fixing a screen reader issue that affects a smaller subset of your audience, the math is brutal and the right answer rarely survives it.

That is the thing AI is quietly changing. And it is more exciting to me than almost anything else happening right now.

The Art Director Is Back

Here is what my workflow looks like right now. My team and I built an agent harness that uses orchestration to fire off its own worker agents, code review agents, and QA agents in a fully automated process. It ends in a pull request that is pretty close to production ready. I do not do much actual coding anymore. I think of myself as a facilitator.

It reminds me of my early career as an Art Director. I had the vision for where the work needed to land and I directed the people who could get it there. That is exactly what this feels like. I set the direction. The agents do the work. And while they are building I am not waiting. I am circling back to see where we landed, finding where we still have room to improve, and writing the next Linear issues to get us there. It is cyclical. It is fast. I am the conductor. And it gives me something I have never had in this job. Room to think.

That recovered capacity does not feel like a small thing when you are inside it. It feels like someone gave you a second set of hands. And the first thing I did with those hands was accessibility work. Real accessibility work.

Focus order. Keyboard navigation. ARIA labels. The unglamorous, detail heavy work that never makes a feature demo and never makes a sprint either. I did not plan it. Nobody scheduled it. I had the capacity, so I did it.

Color contrast is still sitting in the backlog as I write this. That part has not changed. What has changed is that the backlog is actually getting reached. For the first time in my career the queue is moving instead of growing.

The 2.2 Billion People at the Back of the Line

The World Health Organization estimates that at least 2.2 billion people globally live with some form of vision impairment. By 2050 that number is projected to grow significantly. These are people who have always been underserved by traditional visual UI, not because designers did not care, but because the economics of inclusion have always been hard to justify in a roadmap conversation.

Voice input that actually understands natural language. Screen readers that can navigate AI-generated content intelligently. Alternative interaction models built for people who have never been well served by a mouse and a monitor. These are not nice to haves. They are the difference between participating in the digital world and being excluded from it.

And for the first time in my career I am actually getting to work on them. Not plan them. Not push them to the backlog. Work on them.

What AI Is Actually Giving Us

People are going to keep arguing about what AI is taking. That conversation is not going away and it should not. The concerns are legitimate and they deserve serious people working seriously on them.

But I want to make the case for what it is giving back. Because I am living it right now.

AI is giving us time. And that time gets to go towards the things that always mattered but never made it to the top of the list. The edge cases. The inclusive design decisions. The accessibility work that should have shipped three sprints ago. The details that separate a product that works from a product that works for everyone.

The people who have always been at the back of the line might finally be getting to the front. Not because the industry suddenly grew a conscience. Because we finally have the capacity to act on the one we already had.

That is what keeps me excited about this. That is what keeps me at the table.
